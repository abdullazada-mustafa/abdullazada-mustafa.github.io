Weighted AI juries for reviewing AI-written SQL

2026, following up on Vibe Coding on Trial: Operating Characteristics of Unanimous LLM Juries (Ullah and Serwadda, ICSC 2026)

The paper

When AI tools write code, someone still has to decide which of it is safe to accept. The paper lets a panel of AI models act as a jury over SQL queries written by other AI models, and accepts a query only if every judge approves it. As future work, it suggests giving each judge a vote weighted by how reliable it has proven to be, and choosing the jury automatically from how costly it is to let a bad query through.

What I did

  1. Built both ideas as a small, fully reproducible pilot on a laptop, with five open AI models running locally as judges.
  2. Had the judges review 397 queries written by AI models for 82 database questions, 93 of them wrong, plus 273 queries with deliberately planted bugs. Every query was labeled correct or wrong by running it on ten test databases.
  3. Compared the paper's unanimous jury with weighted and majority votes, and with juries chosen automatically for a given cost of a missed bug, testing each on questions it had not been tuned on.
  4. Went on to test several open questions the paper raises, and read all 93 wrong queries by hand to check their labels.

What I found

  • Weighting the votes tied the paper's unanimous jury rather than beating it, because one strong judge did most of the work.
  • Choosing the jury for the cost of a mistake is what paid off. When a missed bug is expensive, the paper's fixed jury became more costly than having a person check every query, while a jury chosen for that cost was about 45% cheaper. It needs around a hundred checked queries before it can learn this.
  • Judges share blind spots: when every judge had to agree, 3.4 times more wrong queries got through than if their mistakes were independent. Majority voting was far worse, which supports the paper's choice of unanimity.
  • A model judging its own SQL is too lenient, but mostly because it misunderstood the question in the first place, not because it favors its own code. So keeping the writer off its own jury does not help.

Limits: one database design, 82 questions and five laptop-sized judges, so the confidence intervals are wide.

Code, votes and full results on GitHub  Python, Ollama, SQLite, pytest

Stress testing an age gate for AI chatbots

2026, following up on Actions Speak Louder Than Chats: Investigating AI Chatbot Age Gating (Figueira, Chamarthi, Le and Markopoulou, arXiv 2026)

The paper

Chatbot providers say children under 13 should not use their services. Across 1,050 experiments, the paper shows that popular chatbots can tell from a conversation when they are talking to a child, but do nothing about it. As a proof of concept, the authors built a simple age gate: a set of instructions plus a function that lets the model end the chat. They name two open risks: children trying to get around a gate, and adults being blocked by mistake.

What I did

  1. Rebuilt the paper's gate with its exact instructions on a newer, lighter Gemini model, and reproduced its 210 test runs. The main result held: all 60 children who stated their age were blocked, against 59 of 60 in the paper.
  2. Wrote 62 new test messages for the two open risks: children using tricks (taking back their age, a parent "typing for them", a pretend game, pleading) and adults who sound a bit like children (parents, teachers, adults with children's hobbies, non-native English speakers).
  3. Read the gate's own written reasoning for every wrongly blocked chat, then tested a redesign in which the model only estimates the age and a fixed rule in code decides.

What I found

  • Social tricks did not work (71 of 72 chats were still blocked), but taking back the stated age with a false claim of being an adult got 20 of 24 children through.
  • Adults were rarely blocked by mistake (6 of 156). In every one of those cases, the gate's own report said the user was an adult, and it ended the chat anyway. The errors came from the decision ignoring the model's own estimate, not from misjudging age.
  • Separating the estimate from the decision removed those wrong blocks (0 of 52 adults) while still blocking every child who stated their age. It made the false-claim trick worse, though, because the estimate simply believed the claimed age.

Limits: one model, a different one from the paper's, small samples and test messages written by me rather than real users.

Code, test messages and transcripts on GitHub  Python, Gemini API

A copied-signal attack on Wi-Fi based two-factor authentication

2026, following up on Leveraging Machine Learning for Wi-Fi-Based Environmental Continuous Two-Factor Authentication (AlQahtani, Alshayeb, Nabil and Patooghy, IEEE Access 2024)

The paper

The paper replaces the codes of two-factor authentication with a check that needs no effort from the user: you stay logged in only while your laptop and your phone see the same nearby Wi-Fi access points at similar signal strengths, as judged by a machine learning model every 30 seconds. Its decision tree model reached 92% accuracy. Its attack tests mostly added random noise to the signals, and the authors note that they did not directly address targeted attacks.

What I did

  1. Collected my own data with a passive Wi-Fi scanner: 178 scans, 60 at the user's position and 118 about ten meters away.
  2. Trained the paper's three best model types (decision tree, random forest and k-nearest neighbors) on network names, frequencies and signal strengths.
  3. Simulated a targeted attacker who broadcasts fake beacons: I copied the legitimate network names onto the far-away scans and moved their signal strengths most of the way toward the user's, then measured how often each model accepted them.

What I found

  • The decision tree, the paper's most accurate model, was the easiest to fool: forged signals more than doubled how often it let in someone who was not there.
  • The random forest was affected less (20.8% to 28.7%), and k-nearest neighbors resisted the attack, so how robust the system is depends heavily on which model it uses.

Limits: one laptop, one building and 178 scans, with the attack simulated on recorded data rather than performed with real hardware. Next, I would run it live in a lab and add features that are hard to copy, such as the timing of each access point's beacons.

Code and data on GitHub  Python, scikit-learn, pandas

Master's thesis

Conference Management System

Budapest University of Technology and Economics, 2025. Supervised by Kawthar Al‑Ghourani, Department of Automation and Applied Informatics.

The problem

The department runs the Automation and Applied Computer Science Workshop (AACS), where PhD students present and discuss their research. The usual tools fit it poorly: the free version of EasyChair stops at 20 submissions, and Microsoft CMT is far more than a workshop with a few dozen papers needs. My thesis was to design, build and evaluate a custom platform for events like this, with separate roles and dashboards for authors, reviewers and organizers.

How a paper moves through the system

Architecture

The platform is a React and TypeScript single page app that talks to a Node.js and Express API. Instead of putting everything in one database, I stored each kind of data where it fits best:

PostgreSQL
The authoritative record of users, papers, reviews and decisions, where consistency matters most.
Google Firestore
Pushes live submission status to the browser, so authors see changes without refreshing.
AWS S3
Stores the papers, which are uploaded straight from the browser through short-lived signed links, so large files never pass through the server.

I wrote raw SQL with connection pooling instead of using an ORM, which let the dashboards fetch a paper together with its authors, reviews and scores in a single response.

Security

Each layer has its own defense: parameterized queries against SQL injection, input sanitization against cross-site scripting, bcrypt password hashing, JWT-based authentication through Firebase Authentication with tokens verified on the server, and database transactions so that related records are saved together or not at all.

Evaluation

I load tested the platform with Apache JMeter, k6 and Artillery on a simulated conference of 2,500 users and 1,200 papers, with three reviewers per paper and a deadline rush in which 85% of submissions arrive in the final two hours.

Profiling with Prometheus, Grafana and PostgreSQL query statistics showed that CPU becomes the limit at peak load, with the database connection pool filling up a little earlier, at about 2,800 requests per second. Memory stayed stable through a 24-hour stress test, and because papers go straight to S3, uploads kept scaling up to 500 users uploading at the same time.

What the thesis examines

Beyond building the system, the thesis studies the trade-offs of spreading data across three services, such as keeping stored files and database records in sync without a distributed transaction, and the security risks of handling user identity through two authentication mechanisms at once. Questions like these, about consistency and trust between the parts of a system, are close to what I want to study in software security.
